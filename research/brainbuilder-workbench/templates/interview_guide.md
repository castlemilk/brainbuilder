## Start with the most recent real event

1. Tell me about the last time you wanted to learn something but had only a few minutes. What triggered it?
2. Where were you? How much uninterrupted time did you actually have? What interrupted you?
3. What did you open or do first? Can you walk through the sequence?
4. What were you hoping to be able to explain or do afterwards?
5. What happened when you came back later? What did you remember without reopening the material?
6. What have you tried to solve this already? Which parts did you keep using or abandon, and why?
7. When does listening work for you? When is it impractical to speak, touch a screen, or listen closely?
8. What learning routine already works well enough that you would not replace it?
9. What have you actually paid for, renewed, or cancelled? What prompted that decision?
10. What would make you stop using a product like this, even if the lessons were good?

Ask for concrete episodes, artifacts the participant chooses to share, and exceptions. Avoid asking “Would you use our AI microlearning app?” as evidence of demand. Do not suggest a feature before understanding the behavior.

## Prototype tasks after discovery

Use a neutral task and observe rather than explain: find a useful three-minute activity; stop halfway; return later; attempt a recall question; change the time or interaction mode; decide whether to continue. Record the first action, hesitations, unassisted task completion, and errors. Keep moderator help separate from successful independent use.

## End with a behavioral next step

Offer an explicitly scoped follow-up study only when outreach and participation are authorized. An actual return, chosen trial, completed delayed check, or clearly priced opt-in is more informative than enthusiastic feedback. Record refusals and nonresponses too. Do not treat a refundable deposit, trial acceptance, or hypothetical price answer as recurring paid retention.
